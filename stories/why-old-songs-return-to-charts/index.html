Why old songs return to music charts — and what a re-entry actually means

A re-entry is not a glitch. Streaming, media moments, tours, anniversaries and renewed discovery can put an older recording back into a current weekly competition.

By Daegon Charts Editorial · October 13, 2026

One of the most visible changes in the streaming era is the disappearance of a hard line between “current” music and catalog. A recording can be years or decades old and still become one of the most-consumed songs of a new week. When that happens, a weekly chart has to decide whether the old title is eligible to return and how its previous history should be preserved.

What RE means on Daegon

Daegon marks a title as RE when the same chart identity appeared before, dropped out and later returns. The old peak and cumulative week count stay attached to the recording. This is different from NEW, which is reserved for the first appearance of that chart identity.

On the Daegon 100 dated October 10, 2026, Rihanna’s “Umbrella” re-enters at No. 26, Britney Spears’ “Toxic” returns at No. 90 and Anitta’s “Ritmo Perfeito” returns at No. 89. Those positions describe current weekly performance, while their RE labels tell the reader that each title already has an earlier Daegon history.

RE = returnThe current week is new; the song’s chart identity is not.

Why catalog consumption suddenly rises

There is rarely one universal cause. A film or television placement can expose a track to a new audience. A tour can revive an artist’s catalog. Social platforms can turn one lyric or production moment into a meme. An anniversary edition can put an album back into circulation. A new release can send listeners backward through an artist’s discography. Sometimes the change is slower: a classic simply accumulates enough renewed activity to clear the weekly threshold again.

Why the original identity matters

The difficult part is not showing the song again; it is deciding whether the returning consumption belongs to the same recording. Daegon’s historical work treats spelling variants, remixes, featured credits and alternate editions cautiously because splitting one recording into multiple chart identities can create false debuts and false peaks.

A remix should not automatically become a separate historic hit if the underlying chart rule credits that activity to the original song. Conversely, a genuinely different recording should not inherit a run it did not earn. Identity cleanup is therefore part of the chart methodology, not merely a cosmetic database task.

Catalog makes weekly charts more historical

When current releases compete with catalog, a weekly chart becomes a snapshot of active listening rather than a release calendar. That can produce unusual combinations: on October 10, 2026, new and recent Ariana Grande songs occupy the top three while “Music” by Madonna is No. 33, “Beat It” by Michael Jackson is No. 61 and several older titles appear deeper in the ranking.

Re-entry does not erase the gap

Daegon can distinguish a continuous run from a return. A title that spends 20 total weeks on chart with a long absence in the middle should not be interpreted as 20 consecutive weeks. The RE marker is the quick visual cue, while the historical page is where the full sequence can be examined.
