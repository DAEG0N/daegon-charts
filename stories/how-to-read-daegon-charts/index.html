How to read Daegon Charts: LW, Peak, Weeks, Re-Entry and weeks at No. 1

A chart row is a compact history. Here is what every movement, peak and week count means — and why the fields should be read together.

By Daegon Charts Editorial · October 13, 2026

A weekly chart looks simple because the first thing the eye sees is a rank. But rank alone says very little about the shape of a hit. Daegon Charts keeps several history fields beside each song, album or artist so a reader can distinguish a debut from a return, a one-week spike from a long run and a former No. 1 from a release still climbing.

Rank

The large number at the left is the entry’s position for the selected chart week. No. 1 is the leader for that week. The rank is not an all-time score and should not be confused with Peak.

LW — Last Week

LW shows the entry’s position on the immediately preceding chart. If a song is No. 4 this week and LW is 9, it moved up five places. A dash normally means the title was not on the previous chart.

Peak

Peak is the highest position the entry has reached across its chart run. A song can be No. 27 this week with a peak of No. 1 because the peak records its best historical rank, not its current one.

Current rank ≠ PeakRank describes this week. Peak describes the best week in the run.

Weeks on Chart

This is the cumulative number of chart weeks credited to the entry. It is useful for separating a new hit from a catalog title with a deep history. A re-entry continues its historical week count rather than starting a completely unrelated run when the identity is the same recording.

Weeks at No. 1

When an entry has reached the summit, Daegon also shows the number of weeks it has spent there. Those weeks do not need to be consecutive. If a song leads, leaves No. 1 and later returns, each summit week adds to the total.

NEW, RE and movement arrows

NEW means an entry appears on that chart for the first time in the tracked history. RE means it previously charted, left the ranking and has now returned. Up, down and static arrows compare the current rank with LW. They do not describe changes in raw consumption by themselves.

Why a movement arrow can be misleading without the numbers

A song can gain consumption and still fall if other songs grow faster. It can also rise while its own units decline if the rest of the field weakens. That is why Daegon shows consumption metrics on eligible modern charts alongside movement.

Read the row as a sentence

Take a hypothetical row: No. 8, LW 14, Peak 3, 22 Weeks, 2 Weeks at No. 1. In one line it says the title rises six places this week, previously reached No. 3 — or, if weeks at No. 1 is present, the peak must in fact be No. 1 and the data should be checked — and has a substantial run rather than a new debut. Reading fields together is also a useful way to spot data inconsistencies that need correction.
