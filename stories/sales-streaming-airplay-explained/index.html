Sales, streaming and airplay on Daegon Charts: why one number never tells the whole story

A chart position is a relative outcome. Sales, streams and airplay describe different kinds of activity, and their balance helps explain why a song rises, falls or holds.

By Daegon Charts Editorial · October 13, 2026

Modern music consumption is fragmented. A listener can buy a track, stream it repeatedly, hear it on radio or encounter it through several channels in the same week. A useful chart cannot treat those actions as identical, but it also cannot understand a song’s reach by looking at only one of them.

Sales

Sales represent direct purchases inside the Daegon model. They are typically smaller in raw count than streams, so the chart formula does not simply add one sale to one stream as if the behaviors were equivalent.

Streaming

Streaming captures repeated on-demand listening and is often the largest visible volume in a modern chart week. Because raw stream totals can be much larger than sales, their contribution is normalized by the Daegon formula rather than allowed to overwhelm every other signal automatically.

Airplay

Airplay measures a different kind of exposure. It can keep a song competitive even when sales have cooled, or reveal that a record’s audience is broadening beyond its core streaming base. On the October 10, 2026 Daegon 100, the leading songs show noticeably different mixes of sales, streams and airplay despite competing near one another.

Position is relativeA song can grow and still fall if the rest of the chart grows faster.

Why percentage changes matter

Daegon displays week-over-week changes beside several metrics. Those percentages explain the direction of the underlying activity, but they should not be read as a prediction of rank movement. A song with a streaming increase may drop because a competitor posts a much larger overall gain. A title with declining raw consumption may rise if the whole field contracts more sharply.

Points and units are not the same thing

Points are a ranking mechanism. Units are a consumption representation. Conflating the two creates false precision: a score designed to order songs should not automatically be described as literal copies sold or literal plays. Daegon keeps those concepts separate in the interface and methodology.

Why historical charts need a different treatment

The farther back the archive goes, the less comparable the underlying consumption environment becomes. A 2001 market should not be forced into a 2026 streaming model merely because the modern fields exist. Historical reconstructions therefore rely on the source framework appropriate to their period and are presented as reconstructed rankings rather than pretending that modern consumption data existed unchanged.

The most useful way to read a weekly row is to combine the current rank, LW, Peak, Weeks, movement and the available consumption signals. No single field tells the whole story.
